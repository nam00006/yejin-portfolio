Applied analysis artifact

Choosing the Right Model: When Machine Learning Beats Deep Learning

A decision-focused report that compares a traditional machine learning approach with a deep learning approach using two real business deployments, and explains why swapping the two would raise cost without improving results.

2 Case Studies SVM vs CNN Decision Framework
Machine LearningCustomer churn
prediction
VS
Deep LearningVisual quality
inspection

Two problems, two very different right answers

Traditional ML — Customer churn prediction

Case: Dialog Axiata, a telecom operator, built churn prediction on structured subscriber data using a managed cloud ML platform.

Why ML fits: the inputs are already clean, labeled columns — tenure, billing, usage, support contacts. Support vector machines and similar models perform strongly on tabular data, train on modest hardware, and produce results a retention team can question and act on.

Why deep learning does not: neural networks routinely underperform tree- and margin-based models on tabular problems while demanding far more data, tuning, and compute — and the loss of interpretability makes retention offers harder to justify.

Deep Learning — Visual quality inspection

Case: BMW Group deployed convolutional neural networks on the production line to detect part defects and verify component fit from camera images.

Why DL fits: the input is raw pixels with no predefined features. CNNs learn the edges, textures, and shapes that signal a defect directly from labeled images, at line speed and at a consistency manual inspection cannot hold across shifts.

Why traditional ML does not: it would require engineers to hand-craft every visual feature in advance, which breaks down the moment lighting, angle, or part variation changes.

The decision rule I took away

The question is not which technique is more advanced. It is whether the useful features already exist in the data. When they do, traditional machine learning is faster, cheaper, and easier to defend. When they must be discovered from raw signal, deep learning earns its cost.

Download full report ↓

Objective

To give operations and supply chain leaders a practical way to decide between traditional machine learning and deep learning before committing budget — grounded in two documented deployments rather than in general claims about which technology is newer.

The academic version of this work asked me to explain the difference between the two approaches. For this portfolio I reframed it around the question a business audience actually asks: which one should we pay for, and how would we know if we chose wrong?

Process

  1. Selected one traditional machine learning problem and one deep learning problem where the data types differ sharply — structured records versus raw images.
  2. Researched a documented real-world deployment for each, rather than relying on textbook illustrations.
  3. Analyzed why each method suits its problem, using data structure, interpretability needs, and compute cost as the deciding criteria.
  4. Argued the reverse case for both — what would go wrong if the approaches were swapped — to test the reasoning instead of only supporting it.
  5. Reframed the finished report for a non-technical operations audience and added a plain-language decision rule.

AI communication artifact

Neural Networks, Explained Like a Sorting Line

A plain-language explanation of how a neural network works, written for a non-technical operations audience using a distribution-center sorting-line analogy instead of mathematics.

Non-technical Audience Analogy-Driven Responsible Framing
Inputs ↓ Hidden Layers ↓ Output

A neural network as a package sorting line

Imagine a package arriving at a distribution center. It travels down a line of stations, and each station asks one small question — how heavy is it, what does the label say, is it fragile. By the end of the line, all those small answers add up to one decision: which truck it goes on. A neural network works the same way.

The parts of the network

Inputs are the clues — weight, size, label. For a demand forecast, they might be recent orders, seasonal trends, and inventory levels.

Hidden layers are the stations in the middle of the line. Each one looks for a more complex pattern than the one before it.

Weights decide how much importance the network gives each clue — a damaged-box label may matter more than the color of the tape.

Output is the final decision: "Send this package to Truck 4," or "Plan to ship 1,200 units next month."

How it learns — and where it stops

Learning works like a new employee. The network makes a prediction, gets feedback on whether it was right, and adjusts how much importance it gives each clue. Repeated across many examples at enormous speed, it gradually becomes more accurate.

The limitation: a network only learns from the examples it has been given. A model trained on last season's shipping patterns can confidently produce an outdated answer when demand, inventory, or transportation capacity changes — which is why human judgment at the end of the line still matters.

Originally written for AIML-500 Discussion 3.4, "Communicating for Learning" (459 words), then reframed for a professional audience.

Objective

To explain a technical AI concept — how a neural network works — clearly enough for a non-technical operations audience to follow, without relying on mathematics or jargon.

The academic version of this work asked me to explain neural networks with the help of a generative AI model. For this portfolio I kept the workplace framing so the explanation reads as something I could hand directly to a warehouse or supply chain team, not only as a classroom answer.

Process

  1. Used chunking — divided the concept into short, single-idea sections: inputs, hidden layers, weights, output, and training.
  2. Used storytelling and analogy — anchored the entire explanation in a distribution-center sorting line the audience already understands.
  3. Used progressive disclosure — explained inputs before layers, weights, and training, so each idea builds on the previous one.
  4. Used relevance and context — connected every part to a familiar workplace instead of starting from abstract mathematics.
  5. Included a limitation on purpose, to encourage critical thinking and two-way discussion rather than presenting neural networks as automatically reliable.

Tools used

Prompt engineering Analogy & audience framing Supply chain domain knowledge Technical communication Generative AI editing support

Artifact-specific value proposition

This artifact demonstrates my ability to translate a technical AI concept into plain language a non-technical audience can act on — a core skill for helping operations teams and leaders adopt AI with real understanding rather than blind trust.

It also shows responsible framing. By naming a concrete limitation, I present AI as a tool that supports human judgment instead of replacing it — the same principle I bring to introducing new tools on the operations floor.

Applied AI reasoning artifact

Thinking in Systems: Three Real-World Data Challenges

A live AI coaching session working through three progressively harder data challenges — missing data, extreme class imbalance, and a real-time recommendation system — reasoning each one as a system decision, not just a modeling choice.

3 Escalating Scenarios Live AI Coaching Production-Level Reasoning
Missing Data ↓ Class Imbalance ↓ Recommender

Three problems, one systems mindset

The coach presented three data challenges of increasing difficulty. For each, I reasoned through my own approach first, then refined it against the coach's feedback.

1 · Missing data that isn't random

A house-price model had 15% of "age of home" values missing — and older homes were likelier to be missing. I flagged this as missing-not-at-random, so deleting incomplete rows would bias the model toward newer homes. Instead: derive age from year built where possible, otherwise group- or model-based imputation on related features, add a missing-value indicator, fit imputation on the training data only to prevent leakage, and evaluate older vs. newer homes separately to catch bias.

2 · 99.95% accurate and still useless

With only 0.05% of transactions fraudulent, a model can score 99.95% accuracy while catching almost no fraud. I dropped accuracy for recall, precision, and PR-AUC; used cost-sensitive learning and controlled resampling; tuned the threshold to business cost instead of 0.5; and designed a tiered response — block high-risk, review medium-risk, monitor low-risk — bounded by the investigation team's real capacity, with an anomaly-detection layer and fairness monitoring on top.

3 · Real-time recommendations at scale

A recommender serving millions of interactions per hour under a 50–100 ms budget, with sparsity, cold start, and concept drift. I designed a two-stage pipeline — candidate generation with two-tower embeddings and approximate nearest-neighbor search, then feature-rich ranking — handled cold start with metadata embeddings, managed drift with recency weighting and champion-challenger testing, and balanced exploration against exploitation with a contextual bandit.

The thread through all three: the most statistically sophisticated solution is not automatically the best one. Every answer had to weigh bias, uncertainty, business cost, latency, fairness, and operational capacity as connected decisions — not isolated technical problems.

Objective

To demonstrate practitioner-level reasoning on the data challenges that break real machine-learning projects — not by naming techniques, but by working through why an approach fits the data, the business cost, and the operational reality.

The graded activity was a live coaching session scored on reasoning and independent critical thinking. For this portfolio I distilled the three scenarios into a decision-focused summary so a professional audience can see how I reason under real constraints.

Process

  1. Worked through three escalating scenarios with an AI data-challenge coach — missing data, class imbalance, and a production recommendation system.
  2. Reasoned each one independently first, then pressure-tested my approach against the coach's feedback.
  3. Extended my answers where the coach raised sharper considerations — adding an anomaly-detection layer and fairness monitoring to the fraud design, and hard-negative mining and position-bias correction to the recommender.
  4. Connected every technical choice back to business cost, operational capacity, and post-deployment behavior.
  5. Summarized the recurring principle — add complexity only where it earns measurable value.
